Operating model

How an Ascent campaign will actually work.

Most donation pages ask for money first and explain later. We're publishing our full operating procedure before we ask anyone for a dollar — so you can judge the process, not just the pitch.

See the first campaign taking shape
1

Identify a verified need 2–4 weeks

A partner clinic tells us what equipment they lack. We confirm the need directly with clinic staff, in writing, and document why it matters for patient care.

  • Written confirmation from the receiving facility
  • Quotes from at least two equipment suppliers
2

Publish the campaign before fundraising

The full budget goes public first: equipment cost, shipping, logistics, and our capped overhead — every line visible before the donate button turns on.

  • A single fixed goal — campaigns don't quietly expand
  • Allocation percentages locked at publication
3

Raise in public live tracker

The progress tracker on this site reflects actual received funds, not pledges. If a campaign stalls, we say so and explain the plan.

4

Purchase and deliver documented

We buy directly from suppliers — funds are never handed off as unrestricted cash. Delivery is confirmed by the clinic and photographed where the partner permits.

5

Report back, line by line within 30 days

Every campaign closes with a public report: receipts, final costs versus estimates, what went wrong, and what we'd change. Boring reports are the point.

Fair questions

Things you should ask a brand-new nonprofit.

You're high school students. Why should anyone trust you with money?

Right now, you shouldn't — trust is earned, and we haven't earned it yet. That's why we aren't accepting donations before registration is complete, why every counter on this site starts at zero, and why our first campaign will be small and fully documented. Judge us on the first report, not this website.

Are you a registered 501(c)(3)?

Not yet. Formal nonprofit registration is in progress, and we will not collect donations until the appropriate legal structure is in place. When status is granted, we'll post the documentation here.

Where would my money actually go?

Each campaign publishes its allocation before fundraising opens — our first campaign's draft budget targets the large majority of funds to equipment purchase, with the remainder for shipping/logistics and a small capped reserve. Ascent is volunteer-run: no salaries, no paid staff.

What happens if a campaign doesn't reach its goal?

The policy we're adopting: funds either roll into the same clinic's next-priority need (with donor notification), or donors are offered the choice at checkout. What never happens is money silently disappearing into "operations."

Why does this site keep saying "zero"?

Because it's true. Plenty of new organizations pad their pages with borrowed statistics or vague "students impacted" claims. We think starting the ledger at zero — in public — is a better foundation than a flattering guess.

Ascent / About
Who we are

A nonprofit young enough to do everything right the first time.

Ascent was founded in 2025 by high school students who kept running into the same two problems: biomedical engineering felt locked behind expensive programs, and the clinics that most needed medical technology had the least access to it. We decided the honest response wasn't a fundraiser — it was an organization.

The story

Why students, why now.

Biomedical engineering is where an idea becomes an infusion pump, a prosthetic, a diagnostic test. It's one of the most consequential fields a student can enter — and one of the least accessible. Most high schools offer no path into it. Most under-resourced clinics can't afford its products.

Ascent exists to work on both ends of that gap at once: teach the field to students who'd never otherwise meet it, and fund the equipment for communities the market skips. We are at the very beginning — and we'd rather show you a small, real thing than a big, imaginary one.

Mission & vision

The two sentences everything else answers to.

Mission

To expand access to biomedical engineering education and healthcare technology for underserved communities — through transparent, student-led programs that document every dollar and every claim.

Vision

A world where the next generation of biomedical engineers comes from everywhere — and where no clinic's standard of care is limited by its zip code.

Core values

Six rules we've written down so you can hold us to them.

01

Transparency by default

Budgets before fundraising, receipts after. If a document can be public, it is public. Our zero-filled impact ledger is this value in practice.

02

Rigor over hype

Technical claims get reviewed before we publish them. Equipment choices follow clinic needs and supplier quotes, not what photographs well.

03

Empathy in practice

Partner clinics define their own priorities. We fund what they say they need — we don't parachute in with what we assume they need.

04

Curiosity, taught

Every program should leave a student knowing more than they did — including our own members, who learn governance, budgeting, and engineering by doing it.

05

Partnership, not charity

We build relationships that outlast a single donation. A campaign is the start of an obligation, not the end of one.

06

Built to outlast us

The founders will graduate. The procedures, documentation, and chapter model are designed so Ascent doesn't depend on any one of us.

Selection criteria

How we'll choose where funds go.

Published now, before any money exists, so no one can accuse us of writing the rules to fit a favorite. A partner clinic must meet all four.

1 · Verified need

The facility documents, in writing, what equipment it lacks and how the gap affects patient care. We confirm with staff directly — not through a middleman.

2 · Underserved population

Priority goes to facilities serving communities with limited healthcare access — measured by the facility's own patient data and public health indicators, not our impressions.

3 · Sustainable use

The clinic must have (or gain, within the budget) staff trained to operate and maintain the equipment. Machines that gather dust help no one — this is a known failure mode of equipment donation, and we plan around it.

4 · Accountability agreement

Partners agree to confirm delivery, report equipment status at set intervals, and allow us to publish outcomes — good or bad.

Ascent / Biomedical Engineering
The field

The engineering discipline that ships to hospitals.

Biomedical engineering (BME) applies engineering methods to medicine and biology — it's how ideas become MRI machines, prosthetic limbs, dialysis systems, and rapid diagnostic tests. This page is our plain-language introduction to the field, plus the free learning track we're building.

Four frontiers

Where BME is moving.

Medical imaging

MRI, CT, ultrasound, and newer portable imaging let clinicians see inside the body without cutting it open. A single reliable ultrasound unit can transform prenatal care at a rural clinic.

  • Physics + signal processing + anatomy
  • Trend: cheaper, portable, point-of-care devices

Biomaterials & tissue engineering

Materials the body accepts: implants, sutures, scaffolds that host growing cells. The core question — how do you build something the immune system won't reject?

  • Chemistry + biology + materials science
  • Trend: 3D-printed scaffolds and implants

Medical devices & instrumentation

Pacemakers, infusion pumps, ventilators, glucose monitors — electromechanical systems where a design flaw isn't a bug, it's a patient-safety event. This is why the field is so rigorous about testing.

  • Electronics + mechanics + regulatory design
  • Most relevant frontier to our equipment campaigns

Health data & computation

Algorithms that flag abnormal heart rhythms, models that help read scans, hospital systems that track outcomes. Software is becoming a medical device in its own right — with the same safety stakes.

  • Programming + statistics + medicine
  • Lowest-cost entry point for students: a laptop

Why it matters here

The access gap, in plain terms.

The education gap
Most high schools offer no biomedical engineering exposure at all — students typically discover the field in university, which means students without engineering-heavy schools or families often never discover it. Our education program attacks this directly.
The equipment gap
Under-resourced clinics — rural, urban, and international — routinely operate without functioning basic equipment, and donated machines frequently fall out of service when maintenance and training aren't part of the plan. That's why our selection criteria require a sustainability plan, not just a purchase.
Why students can help
Not by doing surgery — by doing the unglamorous work: researching equipment options, documenting budgets, teaching fundamentals to younger students, and building the transparent processes adults often skip.
